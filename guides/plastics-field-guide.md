## How to use this guide {#how-to-use}

Chapters 1 and 2 built the skills: name the polymer, read the
manufacture. This chapter is the reference they were building toward —
the house, organized **by plastic**. Each polymer's section lists the
products it dominates, why it won those jobs, what separates a good
specimen from a cheap one, and how it ages. At the end: the safety
rules in one place, and a master table you can consult with a
mystery object in hand.

A theme to watch for: in a mature product category, the polymer choice
is almost never arbitrary. When you find PP in a microwave container
and PET in a soda bottle, you're looking at decades of engineering
natural selection. The corollary is the buying rule this chapter
repeats: **be suspicious of any product wearing the wrong plastic for
its job** — it means the maker optimized for resin price, not for you.

## PET: bottles, jars and fleece {#pet-products}

**Its territory:** single-use beverage bottles, clear food jars
(peanut butter, mayo), salad clamshells, blister packaging — and,
spun into polyester fiber, fleece blankets and most of your athletic
wardrobe.

PET won clear packaging because it's glass's understudy at a fiftieth
of the weight: transparent, strong, and a genuine gas barrier
(Chapter 1's athlete). **Good vs. cheap:** in bottles there's little
range — the category is ruthlessly optimized — but in jars, check the
neck molding for flash and the walls for evenness. **How it ages:**
PET scratches into cloudiness, holds onto flavors and odors, and
creases whitely if flexed. **The replace-when:** it was designed for
one use; refilling a water bottle for a gym week is fine, but hazy,
scratched or flavor-haunted PET is done. It wants to be recycled —
it's the one plastic with a strong afterlife, as Chapter 4 shows — so
let it go.

## HDPE: the utility fleet {#hdpe-products}

**Its territory:** milk and juice jugs, detergent, bleach and shampoo
bottles, buckets, trash cans, cutting boards, coolers' bodies, plastic
lumber and outdoor furniture, kids' playsets.

HDPE is what you buy when the job is *contain something, possibly
aggressive, and survive.* It's the cutting-board plastic because it's
food-safe, knife-kind and dishwasher-tough; it's the bucket plastic
because it doesn't crack under load or cold. **Good vs. cheap:**
weight and wall consistency — a quality bucket flexes evenly and
returns; a cheap one creases white at the rim on the first hard pull.
On cutting boards, thickness is the spec that matters; thin boards cup
in the dishwasher. **How it ages:** slowly and gracefully — UV chalks
it after years outdoors (stabilized grades last decades), and knife
scars on boards eventually harbor stains. **Replace-when:** cutting
boards with deep, discolored knife valleys; otherwise HDPE mostly
outlives its owner's patience.

## PVC: keep it in the walls {#pvc-products}

**Its territory:** drain/waste/vent pipe, siding, window frames, wire
insulation, garden hoses, vinyl flooring, shower curtains, inflatable
pool toys, "vinyl" upholstery.

As construction material, PVC is a champion — cheap, rot-proof,
flame-resistant, fifty-year service life. Our guidance from Chapter 1
stands: **superb infrastructure, poor kitchenware.** Flexible PVC
means plasticizers, and plasticizers migrate — into fatty food on
contact, into the air of a hot bathroom (that new-curtain smell).
**Good vs. cheap:** for hoses and curtains, "phthalate-free" or PEVA
alternatives are the quality marker; for pipe and siding, certification
stamps matter more than feel. **How it ages:** flexible PVC stiffens
and yellows as plasticizer escapes; rigid PVC gets brittle in UV
unless stabilized. **Replace-when:** any flexible vinyl that has gone
stiff, tacky or yellowed — its additives are now elsewhere. And a
standing rule: no PVC in the microwave, ever, and don't buy it where
it will touch fatty food.

## LDPE: the flexible supporting cast {#ldpe-products}

**Its territory:** produce, bread and newspaper bags, cling wraps
(most modern ones), squeeze bottles (honey, condiments), flexible
snap-on lids, freezer bags, the liner in paper cartons.

LDPE gets the roles that need *give*: lids that snap over a rim,
bottles that spring back after a squeeze, films that stretch instead
of tearing. **Good vs. cheap:** thickness is nearly the whole story —
freezer bags versus sandwich bags is a gauge difference, not a
chemistry one. **How it ages:** barely; it's the least drama-prone
polymer in the house. Snap-on lids eventually stretch loose and stop
sealing. **Replace-when:** when a lid no longer bites. One honest
note: its flexibility makes it the recycling system's least favorite
shape — the film problem of Chapter 4 — so buy it where flexibility
is the point, not by default.

## PP: everything that meets heat and food {#pp-products}

**Its territory:** food-storage containers and their lids, yogurt and
deli tubs, bottle caps, straws, baby bottles (with silicone nipples),
electric-kettle bodies, dishwasher interiors, lunch boxes, food
processors' bowls, medicine bottles, reusable "party" cups, lab ware.

This is the section to read before any kitchen purchase. PP holds the
food-plus-heat franchise (Chapter 1's perfectionist), and quality
varies more than in any other polymer because the category is huge.
**Good vs. cheap:** check the lid's living hinge and seal lip for
clean molding; look for even, slightly translucent walls without
regrind specks (Chapter 2's tells); confirm the microwave and
dishwasher symbols are molded in, not stickered on. Weight is signal —
premium containers use thicker walls that resist warping. **How it
ages:** three characteristic ways. It **stains orange** from tomato
and curry (carotenoid pigments dissolve into the polymer with oil and
heat — a brief pre-scrub with cold water and dish soap before heating
helps, and sun-bleaching genuinely reverses it). It gets **brittle in
the freezer** — deli tubs shatter-crack when flexed cold. And UV
**chalks** it outdoors. **Replace-when:** warped rims that no longer
seal, deep scratches or clouding on food surfaces, any brittleness or
cracking. Stains alone are cosmetic, not a safety event.

## PS: the disposable tier {#ps-products}

**Its territory:** foam cups, plates and takeout clamshells (EPS),
disposable cutlery, red party cups, meat trays, CD cases, cheap coat
hangers, some yogurt cups.

Polystyrene products are engineered to a price, and the material's
brittleness is the accepted cost. **Good vs. cheap** barely applies —
within PS the range is thin — the meaningful choice is *whether* PS,
since PP versions of the same products (sturdier cutlery, reusable
cups) cost slightly more and survive many uses. **How it ages:** it
doesn't; it snaps, usually at a molded-in weld line, with that glassy
crack from Chapter 1. **Replace-when:** immediately on any crack —
sharp edges — and note that hot, fatty or citrus-oiled foods soften
and attack it, which is a good reason to keep foam clamshells away
from hot oily leftovers longer than the ride home.

## PC and Tritan: the clear and tough tier {#pc-tritan}

**Its territory:** premium reusable water bottles and drinkware,
blender jars, safety glasses, greenhouse glazing; older baby bottles
and food containers were PC before the market moved.

Polycarbonate is the glass-clear plastic you can hammer on — and the
plastic at the center of the **BPA story**, told fully in the safety
section below. The practical outcome: for drinkware the market moved
to **Tritan**, a copolyester with PC's clarity, no bisphenols, and
better dishwasher endurance. **Good vs. cheap:** in this tier, brand
honesty about the material *is* the quality signal — "Tritan" is a
named ingredient, "BPA-free plastic" unspecified is not. **How it
ages:** the failure mode of the clear-tough family is **crazing** — a
web of fine surface cracks, caused by harsh dishwasher detergents and
citrus oils as much as by time. **Replace-when:** visible crazing or
clouding on any drinking vessel; the cracks are where both bacteria
and leaching begin.

## ABS and friends: the durable-goods shell {#abs-products}

**Its territory:** appliance and power-tool housings, keyboards and
electronics shells, LEGO, vacuum cleaners, luggage shells, remote
controls — usually unmarked or a #7/ABS stamp inside.

ABS is why good appliances feel good: rigid, glossy, impact-tough,
warm to the touch. It's rarely a *choice* you make — it comes with the
device — but it's a quality tell about the whole product: makers who
spend on ABS (or PC/ABS blends) for the shell usually spent inside
too, where bargain goods use brittle PS lookalikes that crack at the
screw bosses. The screwdriver test you can't do in the aisle, the
tap test you can: ABS thuds; PS clicks. **How it ages:** ABS yellows
in sunlight (vintage-computer collectors fight this professionally)
but stays tough for decades. **Replace-when:** it's the device that
dies, almost never the shell.

## Melamine and nylon: the specialists {#melamine-nylon}

**Melamine** (the household thermoset from Chapter 1) makes the
picnic and toddler plates that bounce instead of shattering, and it
earns its place — with two hard rules. Never in the microwave (it
doesn't melt; it degrades, and it heats badly), and treat it as a
serving surface, not a cooking one: regulators' testing found
measurable but within-limits migration into hot acidic food, so soup
bowls for daily scalding use are better in ceramic. Replace melamine
when the glossy surface wears through to a chalky base or chips.

**Nylon** makes the black spatulas and slotted spoons in most kitchen
drawers, plus zip ties, brush bristles and small gears. It's tough
and slick, but its kitchen grades soften near frying temperatures —
a nylon spatula left against a hot pan edge develops that
characteristic melted bevel. When you see the bevel, retire it;
silicone or wood does the hot-pan job better.

## The safety section, honestly {#safety}

Plastic-and-food safety attracts equal parts complacency and panic;
here is the state of it, plainly.

**The mechanism to understand is migration.** Additives and trace
unreacted monomers can move from plastic into food, and three
accelerants do most of the moving: **heat, fat and acid** — worst in
combination, which is why "microwaving leftover curry in a scratched
takeout tub" is the canonical worst case and "cold water in an HDPE
bottle" is nobody's concern. Migration rises steeply with temperature,
with contact time, and with surface damage.

**BPA, the story and the lesson.** Bisphenol A is a building block of
polycarbonate and some can linings; it's weakly estrogen-mimicking,
and its detection in humans drove PC out of baby bottles (banned for
that use in the US in 2012) and most drinkware. The honest sequel:
some "BPA-free" products simply switched to cousin molecules (BPS,
BPF) with similar question marks — which is why the durable advice is
not "buy the sticker" but **prefer polymers that never needed
bisphenols at all: PP, HDPE, Tritan, glass and steel.**

**Phthalates** are the flexible-PVC plasticizers, restricted in toys
since 2008 in the US; the practical residue of that story is the
PVC guidance above — no flexible vinyl against fatty food.

**The working rules, all together:**

1. Microwave only what's *marked* microwave-safe — in practice,
   almost always PP — and prefer glass for high-fat reheating.
   The symbol means the container survives; venting steam and
   short intervals are still on you.
2. Single-use plastics are single-use: PET bottles, thermoformed
   tubs and foam clamshells aren't designed for reheating or long
   reuse.
3. Retire food-contact plastic when it's **scratched, crazed,
   clouded or warped** — damage is where migration and bacteria
   both live. Stains alone are cosmetic.
4. Dishwasher: top rack, and only for items marked for it. The
   bottom rack's heating element is how good containers warp.
5. Freezer: polyethylene and purpose-made PP freezer-ware, not
   deli tubs (they shatter) or PS (never).
6. For babies and daily-driver drinkware, the shortcut is the
   polymer list above — PP, Tritan, glass, steel — and any of them
   beats agonizing over the rest.

## Terms you'll hear {#glossary}

- **Migration** — the movement of additives or monomers from plastic into food; driven by heat, fat, acid, time and surface damage.
- **BPA (bisphenol A)** — a polycarbonate building block with weak hormone-mimicking activity; drove the market shift to PP and Tritan.
- **BPS / BPF** — BPA's substitute cousins in some "BPA-free" products; chemically similar, similarly questioned.
- **Phthalates** — plasticizers that make PVC flexible; restricted in toys, still common in vinyl goods.
- **Tritan** — a bisphenol-free copolyester; the standard premium drinkware plastic.
- **PEVA** — a vinyl-free, chlorine-free shower-curtain material replacing PVC.
- **Crazing** — the web of fine surface cracks that ends the life of clear, tough plastics.
- **Stress-whitening** — the pale crease where a bent plastic has permanently deformed.
- **Food-grade** — resin certified for food contact under FDA regulations; a property of the material *and* its additives.
- **NSF mark** — third-party certification common on food-service gear like cutting boards.
