# T-Shirt Fabrics & Blanks — Reference Guide

*A primer for starting a printed tee business. The blank is half the product — fabric choice determines feel, cost, perceived quality, and which printing methods are even possible.*

---

## 1. Fibers

### Cotton
The default for printed tees. Two specs matter most:

- **Ring-spun vs. open-end (carded / "regular")** — Ring-spun yarn is twisted and thinned during spinning, producing a softer, stronger, smoother yarn. Open-end is cheaper and coarser. Nearly every "soft" premium blank is ring-spun.
- **Combed** — An extra step that removes short fibers, reducing pilling and surface fuzz. *"Combed ring-spun cotton"* is the standard premium spec.

**Upgrades:** Pima / Supima (extra-long staple, very soft, expensive), organic cotton (certification cost, marketing value).

### Polyester
Cheap, wrinkle-resistant, doesn't shrink, wicks moisture. Feels slicker than cotton and can retain odor. **Essential if you ever want to do sublimation printing.**

### Rayon / Modal / Viscose / TENCEL
Semi-synthetic fibers made from wood pulp. Very drapey, silky, cool to the touch. Weak when wet, shrinks unpredictably, adds cost.

### Blends

| Blend | Character | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **50/50 cotton-poly** | The workhorse | Softer than carded cotton, less shrinkage, cheaper |
| **Tri-blend** (~50 poly / 25 cotton / 25 rayon) | "Vintage soft," heathered color | Fashion favorite, harder to print on |
| **Cotton-spandex** | Slight stretch | A few % elastane; common in women's fits |

## 2. Fabric Weight

Measured in **oz/yd²** (US) or **GSM** (metric). Roughly 34 g = 1 oz.

| Range | Feel | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| 3.2–4.3 oz (110–145 gsm) | Light, drapey, semi-sheer | Fashion tees, women's fits |
| 4.5–5.5 oz (150–185 gsm) | Standard mid-weight | Most retail tees |
| 6.0–7.0+ oz (200–240 gsm) | Heavy, structured, opaque | Streetwear, workwear, premium boxy tees |

> Heavier isn't automatically better — it's a style decision. Heavyweight boxy tees are currently trending; lightweight tri-blends read as "soft fashion brand."

---

## 3. Yarn Count (Singles)

Written as **30/1**, **32/1**, **40/1**.

**Higher number = finer yarn = softer, smoother, lighter fabric.**

- 30 singles — common mid-tier
- 40 singles — noticeably finer, holds fine print detail better

This is one of the best single predictors of perceived quality.

---

## 4. Knit Construction

- **Jersey** — Standard single knit; smooth face, looped back. ~95% of tees.
- **Slub** — Yarn deliberately spun with thick/thin variation for a textured, uneven surface. Popular, but the irregular surface can break up fine screen-print detail.
- **Pique** — Textured, waffle-like knit. Mostly polos.
- **French terry / fleece** — For the sweatshirt side of a product line.

---

## 5. Construction Details That Drive Quality Perception

- **Side-seamed vs. tubular** — Tubular is knit as a cylinder: cheaper, but it twists after washing and fits boxier. Side-seamed drapes properly and reads retail. Most premium blanks are side-seamed.
- **Shoulder taping, double-needle hems, ribbed collar with lycra** — Collar stretch-out is the #1 customer complaint on cheap blanks.
- **Finishing** — Pre-shrunk, enzyme washed, or garment dyed. Garment-dyed tees (Comfort Colors style) have soft, slightly faded color and more lot-to-lot variation.
- **Shrinkage** — Expect 3–5% on untreated cotton; near zero on polyester.

## 6. Fabric ↔ Print Method Compatibility

**This is the section that most affects business decisions.**

| Method | Fabric Requirement | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Screen printing** | Anything; best on cotton | Cheapest at volume; high setup cost per color |
| **DTG** (direct-to-garment) | High cotton content (100% combed ring-spun ideal) | Water-based ink must absorb into cotton; polyester won't take it. Great for one-offs |
| **DTF** (direct-to-film) | Works on anything, incl. 100% poly | Adhesive transfer; slight hand-feel; very flexible for small runs |
| **Sublimation** | **Requires polyester**, light colors only | Dye gasses into the poly fiber — permanent, zero hand-feel, but impossible on cotton or dark shirts |
| **HTV / vinyl** | Most fabrics | Good for text and simple shapes; labor-intensive |

### ⚠️ Dye Migration — the classic beginner trap

Polyester dye can sublimate out of the shirt and into your ink during curing — turning white ink pink on a red poly-blend shirt, sometimes days after printing.

**Fixes:** low-bleed inks, poly-blocker underbase, or lower cure temperatures.

## 8. Recommended Next Step

Order a **sample pack of 6–8 blanks** across different weights and blends. Wash them **five times**, then compare.

Hand feel and post-wash behavior — shrinkage, collar recovery, pilling, color fade — are exactly what spec sheets can't tell you.

## Quick Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Blank** | The undecorated garment you print on |
| **Ring-spun** | Softer, stronger yarn spinning method |
| **Combed** | Short fibers removed; less pilling |
| **Singles (30/1)** | Yarn fineness; higher = finer/softer |
| **GSM** | Grams per square meter (fabric weight) |
| **Slub** | Intentionally uneven, textured yarn |
| **Tubular** | Knit as a cylinder, no side seams |
| **Dye migration** | Polyester dye bleeding into printed ink |
| **Underbase** | White ink layer printed beneath colors |
| **Hand-feel** | How the printed area feels to the touch |
